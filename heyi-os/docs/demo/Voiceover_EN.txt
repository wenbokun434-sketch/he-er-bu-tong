A campus cultural festival brings together student organizers, cultural groups, and campus administrators. Everyone wants it to succeed, but they have different expectations about efficiency, fairness, and safety. HeYi OS gives these perspectives a shared process, so common rules have clear reasons and decisions can lead to action. Using the fictional Shared Voices Festival, we will follow one issue from disagreement to a reviewed commitment.

Negotiation begins with understanding each other. Shared perspectives describe each party’s goals, concerns, boundaries, and flexible conditions. The organizers need efficient preparation. Cultural groups want equal opportunities to represent themselves. Administrators need safe venues and clear evacuation routes. These cards are shared case summaries. The facilitator can organize the discussion, but cannot publish a perspective or confirm a decision on another party’s behalf.

Our first issue is stage time and resource allocation. The initial proposal prioritizes groups with the most registrations. We switch to the cultural representative, who objects: smaller groups also need basic presentation time. The facilitator then revises the proposal. Each registered group receives at least one twenty-minute slot, and remaining time is allocated by rotation. The proposal specifies responsibility, action, timing, conditions, and review. The example button only fills a draft. The operator must still check and publish it.

Publishing a revision does not create agreement automatically. We switch between the three demo roles and submit each party’s acceptance separately. Every response applies to the current version. The proposal becomes confirmed only when all three parties accept it. Other topics remain open for negotiation. The group can establish a shared rule without pretending that every difference has already been resolved. This is harmony without uniformity in practice.

Next, we enable live AI assistance in the same rehearsal space. We choose the proposal-completeness check, read the sharing notice, and consent before sending. The assistant analyzes shared material from this case. We can open its citations and inspect the original text behind the analysis. Its role is to help identify missing details, unclear wording, and questions that still need answers. An AI response cannot accept a proposal for anyone. Suggested map links also require human review before they are added to the map.

A confirmed proposal still needs a document that people can act on. In the Agreement workshop, we load the proposal and add a shared purpose, scope, preserved differences, review procedures, and exit arrangements. Saving creates a version; it does not approve it. After the text is frozen for review, all three parties must read and confirm the whole document separately. Acceptance of a proposal is therefore not treated as consent to every newly added clause. The confirmed document becomes the basis for subsequent tasks.

In the Commitment tracker, we create a task from the confirmed document. It has an owner, a different reviewer, a deadline, and acceptance criteria. When the owner submits progress and fictional completion evidence, the task moves to review rather than becoming complete automatically. The designated reviewer checks the material against the criteria and records an acceptance decision. Only then is the task marked as accepted. These are fictional rehearsal materials, but the completion report and the reviewer’s decision are separate actions, both preserved in the commitment history.

The workspace supports Chinese and English, with the same functions and rehearsal workflow in both languages. Switching language preserves progress and drafts, and the agreement can be exported for presentation. HeYi OS makes differences visible, gives each party a voice in the shared rules, and turns those rules into accountable, evidence-based actions that can be reviewed. That is our practical approach to harmony without uniformity.
